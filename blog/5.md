# The Three Evaluators Every AI Product Should Have

A lot of AI products have evaluation systems.

Far fewer have **good evaluation systems**.

The typical starting point is predictable: build an evaluator that measures correctness, detects hallucinations, and maybe checks whether the response follows instructions. Those are useful measurements, but they are only the beginning.

If you're building an AI product for a business, the evaluator should answer a much more important question:

> **Does this response help the business acquire, retain, or recover customers?**

That requires looking beyond the response itself.

I've found it useful to think about AI evaluation through three different user populations:

1. **Current users**
2. **Potential users**
3. **Lost users**

Each group represents a different part of the business, and each needs a different evaluation strategy.

---

## AI Adoption Is Not the Same as AI Hype

Living in Silicon Valley can distort your perception of how quickly AI is actually being adopted.

When everyone around you is using AI coding tools, generating images, building agents, and talking about the latest model release, it is easy to assume the rest of the world is moving at the same speed.

It isn't.

Outside of technology-centric communities, AI adoption can be surprisingly slow. Some people actively reject AI. Others have tried it once or twice and decided it isn't useful. Many people simply don't have a reason to change the way they already work.

This matters when building an AI product because **your potential customers may have very different expectations from your existing AI users**.

### AI should usually be part of the product, not the entire product

One of the biggest risks with an AI startup is building something that can eventually become a feature of a general-purpose AI platform.

If your entire product is essentially:

> "Give us a prompt and we'll use an LLM to do X."

you have to assume that eventually **ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or another general-purpose model could do X directly**.

The model providers have enormous distribution advantages. If the core value of your product is simply an interface around a model, you're competing against companies that own the models and increasingly own the interfaces people already use.

That doesn't mean you shouldn't build an AI product.

It means the AI shouldn't necessarily be the entire product.

The more defensible approach is to combine AI with traditional software:

```text
                    Product
                       │
       ┌───────────────┼───────────────┐
       ▼               ▼               ▼
   Traditional       AI Chat       Automation
    features
       │               │               │
       └───────────────┼───────────────┘
                       ▼
                  User workflow
```

The AI becomes a powerful way to interact with the product rather than the entire reason the product exists.

Your product can maintain the user's data, understand their workflow, execute actions, integrate with existing systems, track history, provide structured outputs, and offer an AI interface when it is useful.

The user isn't paying you because you have access to an LLM.

They're paying because **your product solves a problem**.

---

# 1. Current Users: Optimize What Already Works

Current users are the easiest group to build evaluators for because you already have the most valuable thing an AI system can have:

**real usage data.**

You can observe:

* What users ask
* Which features they use
* How often they use them
* Which responses lead to additional interactions
* Which responses are ignored
* Which responses lead to corrections
* Which workflows are abandoned
* Which users become power users
* Which users eventually stop using the product

This allows you to build evaluators from actual customer behavior rather than hypothetical assumptions.

Imagine an AI writing assistant.

A traditional evaluator might ask:

> "Was this response grammatically correct?"

A product evaluator could ask:

> "Did the user accept the response without editing it?"

An even better evaluator could ask:

> "Did this response contribute to the user completing the workflow?"

Those measurements are much closer to the actual value of the product.

## Usage decline is an evaluation signal

One particularly valuable signal is **decreased usage before churn**.

A customer rarely goes from loving your product to permanently canceling it in a single instant. There can be a period where engagement declines:

```text
Normal usage
████████████████████

Declining usage
██████████████

Pre-churn
██████

Churn
█
```

If you can identify that pattern, you can evaluate the interactions that preceded it.

Perhaps customers who receive a certain type of answer stop using the product more frequently.

Perhaps a workflow became slower.

Perhaps the AI started producing responses that required too much correction.

Perhaps a competitor introduced a feature that made your product less useful.

The evaluator doesn't need to know the answer beforehand. It needs to help you discover the relationship.

## Power Users Are an Especially Valuable Dataset

Power users can be even more valuable because they frequently provide feedback voluntarily.

They're already invested in the product, so they tend to notice subtle problems that casual users never report.

Their feedback can become evaluation data:

```text
User feedback
      ↓
Evaluation example
      ↓
Evaluator
      ↓
New model/prompt
      ↓
Regression test
```

This creates a feedback loop where real users continually improve your evaluation dataset.

The important distinction is that you're not just evaluating whether an AI response is "good."

You're evaluating whether it is **good for the people actually paying you**.

---

# 2. Potential Users: Evaluate for Conversion

Potential users are considerably harder.

You don't have the same amount of behavioral data because they haven't committed to your product yet.

But you can still create useful synthetic evaluators if your onboarding and sales pipelines collect enough information.

This is where **marketing, sales, product, and engineering need to connect**.

Marketing and sales are responsible for getting potential customers to try the product.

Engineering is responsible for making the product good enough that they stay.

If you have thousands of demos or free trials but only 50% convert, you have an enormous evaluation opportunity.

Instead of creating one generic "potential customer" profile, segment potential users according to the factors that actually influence conversion.

For example:

```text
Potential customers

             ┌── Company size
             ├── Job role
             ├── Industry
             ├── Existing tools
             ├── Primary problem
             ├── Usage requirements
             └── Other statistically significant factors
```

You can then create representative customer profiles from the data.

For example:

```text
Profile A
Marketing manager
20-person company
Uses HubSpot
Needs automated reporting
Conversion rate: 72%

Profile B
Founder
3-person startup
Uses spreadsheets
Needs lightweight reporting
Conversion rate: 48%

Profile C
Enterprise analyst
2,000-person company
Uses Salesforce
Needs compliance features
Conversion rate: 31%
```

Your AI evaluator can then simulate how those different customers would react to the same product experience.

## Potential users don't necessarily know what good AI looks like

This is another reason potential-user evaluation is difficult.

Potential customers often have an idea of how AI *should* work based on media, demos, and marketing rather than extensive experience using AI systems.

A person who has never used an AI agent may imagine that it can understand anything, perform arbitrary tasks, and figure out what they mean without clarification.

Then they encounter the actual product.

The AI asks a question.

It makes a mistake.

It doesn't understand an ambiguous instruction.

It needs structured information.

The user's perception can change very quickly.

This creates a difficult product-design tradeoff.

One of the easiest ways to make an AI product feel impressive to a new user is to make it **more willing to assume**.

Instead of asking:

> "Which report do you mean?"

the AI can guess.

Instead of asking:

> "Do you want this sent to the customer or saved as a draft?"

it can choose one.

That can make a demo feel dramatically better.

But it can also reduce accuracy.

```text
More assumptions
      ↓
Less friction
      ↓
Better first impression

      BUT

More assumptions
      ↓
More incorrect actions
      ↓
Lower reliability
      ↓
Potentially worse retention
```

A potential customer might prefer an AI that confidently guesses.

A power user might prefer an AI that asks one additional question rather than making a costly mistake.

Both preferences are legitimate. They're just optimizing for different stages of the customer lifecycle.

This is exactly why potential-user evaluators and current-user evaluators should be separated.

---

## Not Every Potential User Is Convertible

This may be the most important point.

A 100% conversion rate isn't a realistic goal.

Most people who encounter a product will probably never become customers, regardless of how good it is.

Some don't have the problem.

Some don't have the budget.

Some already use another product.

Some don't trust AI.

Some simply don't care.

Some are not willing to change their workflow.

And some are simply not your customer.

Trying to convert all of them can cause you to make the product worse for the people who actually want it.

The objective isn't:

> **Convert everyone.**

It's:

> **Maximize conversion among the people for whom the product creates meaningful value.**

This is especially important for AI products because **AI itself is not necessarily enough to overcome adoption friction**.

A person may like the idea of your AI product and still never change their behavior to use it.

This is why embedding AI into an existing workflow can be so powerful. Gemini being integrated into Google's ecosystem and AI functionality being integrated into products such as Tesla vehicles demonstrate a fundamentally different distribution model: the user doesn't necessarily have to decide to adopt a new AI product. AI appears inside something they already use.

```text
Existing behavior
      ↓
Existing product
      ↓
AI capability
      ↓
User adopts AI
```

That's dramatically different from:

```text
User
  ↓
Hear about AI product
  ↓
Create account
  ↓
Learn interface
  ↓
Trust AI
  ↓
Change workflow
  ↓
Become customer
```

The first approach has much less adoption friction.

The second requires the customer to fundamentally change behavior.

This is why distribution can be just as important as model quality.

---

## Don't Make Up Demographics

This is where statistical discipline matters.

If your data shows that job role has a meaningful relationship with conversion, use it.

If your data shows that someone's favorite color has a 0.3% correlation with conversion, don't put it into the evaluator.

**Don't overfit your evaluator.**

The goal isn't to create an infinitely detailed fictional customer.

The goal is to create a representative model of the customers you actually encounter.

A good evaluator should reflect meaningful patterns in your customer population, not every correlation you can find.

---

# Your Conversion Rate Becomes a Calibration Target

Suppose you have:

```text
10,000 potential users
7,000 converted

Conversion rate = 70%
```

Your synthetic potential-user evaluators shouldn't all magically produce a 70% conversion score.

Instead, the 70% should act as a **baseline**.

Different customer profiles and different product experiences should move the estimated probability up or down.

Conceptually:

```text
Baseline conversion
        70%
         │
    ┌────┴────┐
    ↓         ↓
Positive    Negative
signals     signals
    │         │
    └────┬────┘
         ↓
Estimated conversion probability
```

You can then compare the evaluator's predictions with actual conversion behavior.

If your evaluator predicts 70% conversion for a population and that population actually converts at 69%, you've got a reasonably calibrated model.

If it predicts 70% and reality is 43%, the evaluator needs work.

This distinction is important because an LLM-generated score is not automatically a statistically meaningful probability.

---

# Price Is a Particularly Difficult Variable

There is another complication: **price**.

Someone might tell you they didn't convert because your product costs $100/month.

But what does that actually mean?

Sometimes it genuinely means:

> "I cannot afford $100/month."

There isn't much your product can do about that.

But sometimes "too expensive" means:

> "Your product isn't worth $100/month to me."

Those are very different problems.

For a high-volume, low-ticket SaaS business, it can be useful to normalize for price experimentally.

For example, you might use randomized discounts:

```text
Group A → $100
Group B → $80
Group C → $60
```

If conversion increases dramatically as the price falls, price sensitivity is probably meaningful.

But if a 20% discount barely changes conversion, the problem may not actually be price.

It may be that the product isn't delivering enough value.

Randomized discounts aren't appropriate for every business, but they can provide useful information for high-volume products where you have enough customers to conduct statistically meaningful experiments.

---

# You Can Also Build Evaluators From Real People

Not every evaluator needs to come from analytics.

Sometimes the best evaluator is a real person.

If you know people who fit your target market, you can ask them to use the product and explain why they wouldn't buy it.

Those interactions can become realistic customer profiles.

For example:

> "I understand what this product does, but I wouldn't use it because the workflow takes too many steps."

That is extremely valuable evaluation data.

You can turn it into a profile representing a customer who has high product fit but low tolerance for friction.

Then your AI evaluator can test whether future versions of the product address that problem.

---

# 3. Lost Users: Learn From Churn

Lost users are the third category.

Ironically, they may be the group with the most historical data.

You may know:

* What they used
* How often they used it
* Which features they used
* What they paid
* How long they were customers
* Which AI responses they received
* What their usage looked like before churn

What you often **don't** know is why they left.

That's the hard part.

---

## "Too Expensive" Isn't the End of the Analysis

Pricing is one of the most common explanations for churn.

But I think there's a more useful interpretation:

> If the customer says you're too expensive, ask whether the product provides enough value to justify the price.

You can't capture every customer.

Even utilities don't have complete market capture. Some people live off-grid. Some customers will always choose cheaper alternatives. Some simply won't be able to afford your product.

That's fine.

The goal isn't complete market capture.

The goal is to make your product the obvious choice for the customers who **can** benefit from it.

Adobe is a good example.

There are cheaper alternatives to many Adobe products. But Adobe has built products that become extremely valuable to serious designers, photographers, video editors, and other professionals.

For those users, the question isn't:

> "Is there a cheaper alternative?"

It's:

> "Is the additional value worth paying for?"

That's a much stronger position.

---

# Build Lost-User Profiles From Churn Data

Exit questionnaires can help.

Return discounts can help normalize for price sensitivity.

Usage trends can identify what customers did immediately before leaving.

Power-user churn is particularly important.

If casual users leave, that may be noise.

If your most engaged customers suddenly start leaving, you should pay very close attention.

Those users have already demonstrated that they understand your product and derive substantial value from it.

If they're leaving, something may be fundamentally wrong.

You can build lost-user evaluators around those patterns:

```text
Lost-user profile

High usage
↓
Feature X heavily used
↓
Feature Y introduced
↓
Usage of X declines
↓
Support complaint
↓
Usage declines
↓
Cancellation
```

The evaluator can then test whether a new product experience would have prevented that sequence.

---

# Re-Conversion Is a Separate Problem

Reducing churn isn't the end of the lifecycle.

Once you've improved the product, you can attempt to bring old customers back.

This is difficult, but it happens.

Apple Maps is a useful example of a product that improved substantially after an initially poor launch. Pokémon GO similarly demonstrated that a product can experience renewed engagement through substantial feature and experience improvements.

The important point isn't that every churned customer will return.

They won't.

The point is that **your old customer base is a potentially valuable distribution channel for a better product**.

After improving the reasons customers left, sales and marketing can bring those customers back into the funnel.

---

# Better Onboarding Creates Better Evaluators

All three evaluator categories become dramatically more useful when you have good onboarding data.

You don't want signup to become a 30-question interrogation.

Every additional field introduces friction.

But a few carefully chosen questions can be extremely valuable.

For B2B software, something as simple as:

* Job role
* Company size
* Industry
* Primary use case
* Existing solution

can provide substantially more useful segmentation than collecting dozens of superficial demographic fields.

The goal is:

> **Maximum information with minimum signup friction.**

---

# Pay Users for Their Data

One of the most effective ideas I've seen is treating user insight as a transaction.

Give users something valuable in exchange for information.

For example:

```text
Complete a 5-minute product interview
            ↓
       500 credits
```

or:

```text
Tell us why you didn't use feature X
            ↓
       Free usage
```

Users voluntarily trade information for product value.

For a software company, the marginal cost of providing those credits may be tiny compared with the value of the resulting data.

That can give you a continuous stream of:

* Qualitative feedback
* Customer profiles
* Product objections
* Feature requests
* Churn reasons
* Conversion insights
* Evaluation examples

You're effectively buying high-quality training and evaluation data with compute instead of cash.

---

# Don't Collapse Everyone Into One Evaluator

This is where the three-category approach becomes particularly powerful.

Suppose you have two hypothetical features.

### Feature A

```text
Potential-user conversion
50% → 60%

Current-user retention
60% → 61%
```

### Feature B

```text
Potential-user conversion
50% → 51%

Current-user retention
60% → 70%
```

At first glance, Feature B looks dramatically better.

But now consider your population:

```text
Potential users: 10,000
Current users:    1,000
```

Feature A affects:

```text
10,000 potential users
× 10% conversion improvement
= 1,000 additional conversions
```

Feature B affects:

```text
10,000 potential users
× 1% conversion improvement
= 100 additional conversions
```

while producing a significant retention improvement among the existing 1,000 customers.

Which feature is more valuable depends on customer lifetime value, revenue, costs, and retention duration—but now you're actually evaluating the business rather than merely comparing two AI quality scores.

If you had collapsed both populations into one generic "customer" evaluator, you might completely miss this distinction.

---

# The Business Is a Funnel, Not a Single User

The real system looks something like:

```text
             Potential Users
                   │
                   │ Conversion
                   ▼
             Current Users
                   │
                   │ Retention
                   ▼
              Power Users
                   │
                   │ Churn
                   ▼
               Lost Users
                   │
                   │ Re-conversion
                   └──────────────┐
                                  │
                                  ▼
                           Current Users
```

Each transition has different economics.

Each transition has different failure modes.

And therefore, each transition deserves different evaluation criteria.

---

# The Evaluation Stack

A mature AI product could eventually have something like:

```text
                         AI Response
                              │
            ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
            │                 │                 │
            ▼                 ▼                 ▼
       Correctness        Safety          Instruction
       Evaluator          Evaluator        Evaluator
            │
            └─────────────────┬─────────────────┘
                              │
                    Business Evaluators
                              │
             ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
             │                │                │
             ▼                ▼                ▼
        Current User     Potential User     Lost User
          Evaluator        Evaluator         Evaluator
             │                │                │
             ▼                ▼                ▼
          Retention        Conversion          Churn
             │                │                │
             └────────────────┼────────────────┘
                              ▼
                       Business Impact
```

The first layer answers:

> **"Is the AI response good?"**

The second layer answers:

> **"Is the AI response good for the business?"**

Those are not the same question.

---

# Don't Overfit the Evaluators

There is one rule that applies to everything above:

## **Do not overfit your evaluators.**

It's easy to create incredibly detailed synthetic customer profiles that perfectly explain your historical dataset.

That doesn't necessarily mean you've built a useful evaluator.

You want profiles that generalize.

Your evaluator should represent meaningful patterns in the population without encoding every historical accident.

The same principle applies to conversion rates, churn patterns, demographics, pricing experiments, and user behavior.

The goal isn't to perfectly explain the past.

The goal is to build a model that helps you make better decisions about the future.

---

# The Real Advantage: Connecting the Data

When these systems are connected to your sales, product, and data pipelines, you can start evaluating product changes according to their actual business impact.

Instead of asking:

> "Did the new model improve our AI score?"

you can ask:

> "Did the new model improve conversion among our highest-value potential customers?"

Or:

> "Did it reduce churn among power users?"

Or:

> "Did it address the reasons our highest-value customers historically left?"

That is a much more useful evaluation framework.

Don't optimize your AI product around the assumption that everyone is an ideal customer, that everyone wants AI, or that every non-conversion is a product failure.

**Potential users tell you how to grow.**

**Current users tell you how to retain.**

**Lost users tell you what went wrong.**

And the best evaluation system recognizes that these are three different problems.
