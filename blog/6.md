# An Agile Life

One of the most useful things I've taken from my career as a software engineer has nothing to do with writing software.

It is **Agile**.

More specifically, I've found that applying Agile principles to my personal life—using Jira as the system—has changed how I think about goals, responsibilities, priorities, and even rest.

I don't use it exactly the way a software team would. My personal Jira board rotates between a **Scrum board and a Kanban board**, depending on how much structure I feel I need.

The result is essentially a personal operating system.

---

## Turning Life Into a Backlog

The basic structure is surprisingly intuitive.

I treat:

* **Epics** as large life goals or ongoing projects
* **Stories** as descriptions of the outcomes or states I want
* **Tasks** as the actual things I need to do

For example:

```text
Epic: Financial Wellness

    Story: Build a stronger emergency fund

        Task: Review monthly expenses
        Task: Cancel unused subscriptions
        Task: Adjust automatic savings
        Task: Compare insurance rates
```

Each task can have its own:

* Priority
* Effort estimate
* Duration
* Deadline
* Location
* Dependencies

The dependency system is particularly useful.

---

# Stop Thinking About Everything You Have to Do

Before using an Agile system personally, my thought process around responsibilities was often something like:

> I need to do X.

But then:

> Before I can do X, I need to do Y.

And:

> Before I can do Y, I need to do Z.

Then I would mentally keep track of all three things.

That creates a strange form of background stress. Even if I'm not actively working on something, I'm still thinking about it because I'm afraid I'll forget the chain of things required to eventually complete it.

Agile changed that for me.

If something is in my backlog, I don't need to continuously think about it.

I know it's there.

If it has dependencies, those dependencies are explicitly represented.

If I'm working on the dependency, I'm making progress toward the eventual task.

If it's not a priority, it can remain in the backlog.

The mental difference is substantial:

> **I don't have to remember everything. I only have to work on what's currently actionable.**

The backlog becomes externalized memory.

---

# Tasks Have Constraints

One of the biggest differences between a personal to-do list and my Jira system is that a task isn't just a sentence.

I attach constraints to it.

For example:

```text
Task: Replace broken equipment

Priority: High
Effort: 3
Duration: 45 minutes
Location: Home
Deadline: Friday
Dependencies: Research replacement
```

This allows me to reason about tasks in terms of actual capacity.

A task that takes 15 minutes is fundamentally different from one that takes three hours.

A task I can do anywhere is different from one that requires me to be at home.

A task that depends on another task shouldn't be treated as immediately actionable.

Once those properties are visible, prioritization becomes much easier.

---

# Agile Helps Me Maintain Balance

There's another reason I find this useful that has less to do with productivity.

I'm prone to going all-in on one thing.

If I become particularly interested in a project, I can spend enormous amounts of time on it while unintentionally neglecting other parts of my life.

Then eventually I look around and realize:

> I've made tremendous progress on this one goal, but I've neglected everything else.

Tracking multiple life goals makes that imbalance visible.

I can see the work I'm doing across different epics rather than evaluating my life based on whatever happens to occupy my attention that week.

For example:

```text
Personal
├── Physical Health
├── Financial Wellness
├── Career
├── Relationships
├── Learning
└── Personal Projects
```

The point isn't to make every category perfectly balanced.

The point is to notice when one category has effectively disappeared.

---

# Jira for the Unpredictable, Calendar for the Routine

One important distinction I've made is between **Agile tasks and routines**.

I don't use Jira to manage every minute of my day.

For routines, I prefer a digital calendar.

Things like:

* Sleep
* Work
* Exercise routines
* Regular appointments
* Recurring commitments

belong naturally on a calendar.

Jira is much better for things that are:

* Unpredictable
* One-off
* Multi-step
* Dependent on other work
* Difficult to estimate
* Competing for limited capacity

A calendar answers:

> **When am I doing this?**

Agile answers:

> **What should I be doing, and what needs to happen first?**

Those are different problems.

# Sprints Give Me a Time Boundary

I don't always use Scrum.

Sometimes Kanban makes more sense because I simply want to continuously pull the next important task.

Other times, I use a sprint because I need to stop and ask:

> **What am I actually trying to accomplish over this period?**

That review is useful because priorities change.

Something that seemed extremely important two weeks ago may not matter anymore.

Something sitting at the bottom of the backlog may suddenly become urgent.

A sprint gives me a forcing function to reassess those priorities.

It's less about completing every task and more about making sure the work I'm doing reflects what I currently care about.

# Effort Estimates Became a Personal Analytics Tool

One unexpected benefit has been collecting enough historical data to understand my own limits.

If I estimate effort and duration consistently, I can eventually compare:

```text
Estimated effort
        vs.
Actual effort
```

and:

```text
Estimated duration
        vs.
Actual duration
```

Over time, I can learn things like:

* How many difficult tasks I can realistically complete in a week
* How long certain categories of work actually take
* How much context switching affects me
* How much discretionary capacity I have
* Which types of tasks I consistently underestimate

This can be analyzed at different time scales.

I can look at my capacity across an entire work week.

But I can also get much more specific.

For example:

> How much effort can I realistically handle between finishing work and going to sleep?

That question is surprisingly useful.

Without measuring it, it's easy to keep adding things to an evening because each individual task seems manageable.

Eventually, the aggregate workload becomes unreasonable.

---

# This Helps Prevent Overwork

Productivity systems can easily become productivity traps.

If the objective is simply:

> **Complete as many tasks as possible.**

then the system has no reason to tell you to stop.

You can always add another task.

You can always increase your sprint commitment.

You can always work longer.

That's not sustainable.

Tracking effort and duration gives me a way to see where my limits actually are.

The goal isn't to maximize utilization.

It's to understand capacity well enough that I can operate within it.

In software engineering, we wouldn't normally expect a system to operate indefinitely at 100% resource utilization.

People shouldn't either.

---

# Some Epics Never End

My use of epics also differs from how I would typically use them at work.

In a software organization, an epic generally has an expected endpoint.

Eventually the feature ships.

The project finishes.

The epic closes.

Some personal epics don't work that way.

For example:

```text
Epic: Physical Health
```

There may never be a point where I can confidently say:

> Physical health: complete.

The same applies to:

```text
Financial Wellness
Career
Relationships
Learning
```

These are continuous areas of improvement.

I might complete dozens of tasks within them, but completing one task simply creates another opportunity.

That doesn't make the system broken.

The objective isn't necessarily completion.

It's **continuous improvement**.

---

# The Long-Term Goal Is to Close the Endless Epics

There is, however, an interesting possibility.

Some of these perpetual epics could eventually be converted into stable systems.

For example, instead of repeatedly creating tasks around some aspect of financial management, I could eventually create an automation that handles most of it.

Instead of manually managing a recurring process, I could build a system that makes the process automatic.

The progression becomes:

```text
Manual task
     ↓
Repeated task
     ↓
Recognized pattern
     ↓
Automation
     ↓
Stable system
     ↓
Epic becomes less active
```

The ultimate goal isn't to have an enormous Jira backlog for the rest of my life.

It's to gradually turn recurring problems into systems that require less active management.

---

# Eventually, I Want an Agent to Run the System

The next logical step is automation.

Jira has an API.

Google Calendar has an API.

That creates an interesting possibility: an agent that manages the relationship between my backlog and my calendar.

Conceptually:

```text
             Jira
              │
              │ Tasks
              ▼
        Personal Agent
              │
       ┌──────┴──────┐
       │             │
       ▼             ▼
   Priorities     Capacity
       │             │
       └──────┬──────┘
              ▼
        Google Calendar
```

The agent could potentially look at:

* Current priorities
* Task dependencies
* Estimated effort
* Deadlines
* Available calendar time
* Location requirements
* Existing routines
* Historical completion rates

and create realistic sprints and calendar blocks.

Instead of me manually deciding:

> "When should I do all of this?"

the system could make a reasonable proposal based on how I actually work.

The important distinction is that I don't want an agent deciding what my life should be.

I want it to handle the **scheduling and administrative complexity** of a system whose priorities I define.

# Agile Isn't About Moving Faster

The biggest lesson I've taken from using Agile personally is that the framework isn't really about productivity.

It's about **reducing cognitive load while making progress visible**.

A traditional mental to-do list forces you to remember:

* What needs to happen
* What depends on what
* What's important
* What can wait
* When something is due
* How long it will take
* Where it needs to happen

An Agile system externalizes that information.

```text
Your brain
    │
    │ priorities
    ▼
Backlog
    │
    │ dependencies
    ▼
Tasks
    │
    │ capacity
    ▼
Sprint
    │
    │ scheduling
    ▼
Calendar
```

The system carries the complexity so I don't have to.

And perhaps the most useful part is knowing that something doesn't need my attention simply because it exists.

If it's in the backlog, it isn't forgotten.

If it has dependencies, they're documented.

If it's in progress, I'm working on it.

If it's not important enough to enter the sprint, I don't need to think about it.

That has made Agile useful to me for something software teams rarely advertise it as:

**building a calmer and more deliberate way to manage a life that will never have a finished backlog.**
