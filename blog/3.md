# The Frontend Is Becoming the Runtime: Designing Apps for an Upgrade-Driven World

## Mobile Is No Longer a Secondary Platform

The argument for putting more computation into frontend applications becomes much stronger when we look at how people actually access the internet.

In 2025, mobile phones accounted for approximately **58.5% of global web traffic**, according to StatCounter data. That means mobile was not merely a secondary way of accessing the web. It represented the majority of web page traffic worldwide.

The geographic variation is even more important.

In August 2025, mobile accounted for approximately:

| Market         | Mobile share of web traffic |
| -------------- | --------------------------: |
| India          |                       65.5% |
| Brazil         |                       60.9% |
| United Kingdom |                       56.8% |
| United States  |                       49.5% |

These figures come from StatCounter data compiled by DataReportal.

This makes the traditional assumption that the browser is primarily a desktop environment increasingly difficult to justify.

The correct architecture depends on the product and market, but for a large number of applications the mobile device is the primary computing environment for the user.

That changes where computation should happen.

---

# Mobile Hardware Is Also Becoming More Capable

The argument for treating mobile devices as serious computing platforms is not based solely on usage statistics.

The hardware itself has changed.

Modern smartphones contain increasingly powerful CPUs, GPUs, large amounts of memory, fast storage, dedicated machine-learning accelerators, and sophisticated operating-system-level resource management.

At the same time, the installed device base is continually being refreshed.

The iPhone provides a useful example.

There is no reliable public dataset showing the exact global installed-base percentage for every individual iPhone model in 2025, so it would be misleading to claim something like "X% of iPhones were iPhone 15s or newer."

However, sales data gives us a useful proxy for the direction of the market.

In the U.S. March 2025 quarter, the iPhone 16 family accounted for **74% of all iPhone sales**. The standard iPhone 16 represented 20% of sales, while the iPhone 16 Pro and Pro Max together represented 38%. The newly introduced iPhone 16e accounted for another 7%.

By the June 2025 quarter, the iPhone 16e had grown to **11% of U.S. iPhone sales**, while older legacy models fell to just 15%, compared with 28% in the same quarter of 2024.

This does not mean that 85% of iPhones in use were new models. Sales and installed base are different measurements.

It does, however, demonstrate how quickly the hardware available to new buyers can shift toward newer generations.

The global market shows a similar pattern. Counterpoint reported that the **iPhone 16 was the world's best-selling smartphone in 2025**, while Apple occupied seven of the global top ten smartphone model positions.

---

# The Hardware Distribution Is Not Uniform

The iPhone market also demonstrates why application developers should avoid thinking about "mobile users" as one homogeneous group.

Different markets have dramatically different device mixes.

Counterpoint's March 2025 data, for example, found that in the United States the iPhone 16 Pro Max and iPhone 16 each represented 11% of total smartphone sales, followed by the iPhone 16 Pro at 8%.

Japan looked very different. The iPhone 16e represented 21% of smartphone sales, followed by the iPhone 16 at 18% and the iPhone 15 at 15%.

This matters because a hardware strategy that makes sense for a U.S.-centric application may not make sense for a globally distributed application.

The correct question is not:

> "How powerful are smartphones?"

It is:

> **"How powerful are the devices used by our particular users?"**

---

# Upgrade Culture Creates a Moving Hardware Baseline

The important phenomenon is therefore not simply that people own powerful phones.

It is that the hardware baseline is continuously moving.

Consider a simplified model:

```text
2022
Older hardware dominates
        ↓
2023
New generation enters market
        ↓
2024
More capable hardware becomes common
        ↓
2025
Another generation replaces part of the installed base
        ↓
2026+
The process repeats
```

A developer building an application today is not necessarily targeting the same hardware distribution as a developer building the same application five years ago.

This creates a moving target.

The minimum viable hardware for a mainstream application can gradually increase as consumers replace older devices.

There is a natural economic filtering mechanism as well.

Users with very old hardware may eventually experience degraded performance or stop using applications that demand more from their devices. Meanwhile, users who upgrade regularly migrate toward newer hardware.

That does not eliminate the responsibility to support older devices.

It means that **hardware support should be an economic and product decision rather than an arbitrary technical requirement**.

---

# Compatibility Is a Market Decision

Different products should establish different hardware floors.

For a social network, messaging platform, search engine, or other network-effect-driven product, supporting older hardware can be extremely valuable.

Every additional supported device can represent another potential user.

For these products:

> **Compatibility is a growth strategy.**

A specialized B2B application has a different incentive structure.

Businesses frequently purchase and manage employee hardware. They can establish minimum hardware requirements, standardize operating systems, and replace devices through corporate IT programs.

For those applications, targeting a more capable hardware baseline may have a much smaller effect on the addressable market.

The hardware strategy should therefore follow the economics of the product.

# Mobile Usage Makes Frontend Computation More Important

The combination of mobile's share of internet usage and increasing device capability creates a strong case for reconsidering where computation happens.

The old model was often:

```text
User
 ↓
Frontend
 ↓
Network
 ↓
Server
 ↓
Processing
 ↓
Response
 ↓
Frontend
```

A more capable client can instead use:

```text
User
 ↓
Frontend
 ↓
Local processing
 ↓
Result
```

Or, more realistically:

```text
User
 ↓
Frontend
 ↓
Device assessment
 ↓
┌───────────────┬───────────────┬───────────────┐
│               │               │
Weak            Medium          Powerful
│               │               │
Server          Hybrid          Local
│               │               │
└───────────────┴───────────────┘
```

This is the architecture that becomes possible when the frontend is treated as a computing platform.

---

# The Goal Is Not "Everything on the Client"

There is an important distinction.

The argument is not that every application should move as much computation as possible onto the device.

The argument is that **developers should stop assuming that computation belongs on the server by default**.

The appropriate location depends on the workload.

A weak device might receive:

```text
Server-heavy workload
```

A medium device might receive:

```text
Hybrid workload
```

A powerful device might receive:

```text
Local-first workload
```

The API remains responsible for authoritative data, authentication, authorization, persistence, and coordination.

The frontend becomes responsible for as much computation as the device can efficiently handle.

---

# Device Capability Should Become an Input to Architecture

A sophisticated application can eventually make its execution strategy dependent on several variables:

```text
Device capability
+
Available memory
+
Memory pressure
+
CPU capability
+
Network quality
+
Battery state
+
Workload complexity
+
Privacy requirements
+
Latency requirements
```

This produces a more dynamic model:

```text
                    Workload
                       │
                       ↓
                Device assessment
                       │
          ┌────────────┼────────────┐
          ↓            ↓            ↓
        Weak         Medium       Powerful
          │            │            │
          ↓            ↓            ↓
       Server        Hybrid       Local
```

The application is no longer designed around a single execution environment.

It is designed around **capability-aware execution**.

---

# The Broader Trend

The statistics support a larger architectural shift.

Mobile devices accounted for roughly **58.5% of global web traffic in 2025**.

At the same time, newer smartphone generations increasingly dominate new device sales. In the U.S. March 2025 quarter, the iPhone 16 family alone represented 74% of iPhone sales, while legacy iPhones represented a shrinking share of purchases.

And this is happening while the capabilities of smartphones continue to increase.

The result is a world in which the frontend is increasingly capable of performing work that previously would have been considered server-side computation.

The architecture of modern applications should reflect that reality.

The future is unlikely to be purely client-side or purely server-side.

It is more likely to be adaptive:

> **Run the workload wherever the combination of device capability, network conditions, privacy, latency, and cost makes the most sense.**
