# Terraform, AWS, and the Economics of Moving Fast

The most impressive combination of infrastructure tools I have used is **Terraform and AWS**.

Cloud infrastructure used to require a lot of specialized knowledge. You had to scour documentation, memorize configuration options, and understand the exact sequence of steps required to provision services through a console or CLI.

Terraform changes the learning curve.

Instead of memorizing every configuration detail, you can work at a much higher level. You can understand the underlying concepts—networks, queues, databases, compute, permissions, storage—and express what you want as infrastructure code. The implementation details become something you can look up when necessary rather than something you need to memorize.

That has a major consequence for product development: **speed**.

## The Value of Launching Today

When you're trying to launch a product or feature quickly, managed cloud services can remove enormous amounts of setup work.

You can go from an idea to a working production feature on the same day by combining Terraform with AWS's managed services.

That doesn't mean every AWS service is the right choice.

The question I ask is:

> **How much does this service cost, and how long would it take me to build and operate the alternative?**

Those two variables determine whether a managed service makes sense.

For example, I prefer **Expo Application Services (EAS)** over building my mobile CI/CD infrastructure around AWS CodeBuild. EAS solves the problem with very little service management, and its cost is relatively low compared with the engineering time required to build and maintain the equivalent system yourself.

On the other hand, something like **Amazon SNS** can be incredibly fast to adopt. Setting up a notification system with SNS can take minutes. Building and maintaining the equivalent functionality yourself—or negotiating a contract with another managed provider—can take considerably longer.

The economics are different for every service.

## Managed Services Are About More Than Price

It's tempting to compare cloud services exclusively by their infrastructure costs.

That's the wrong comparison.

The real equation is:

**Managed service cost + engineering overhead**

versus

**Self-hosted infrastructure cost + engineering overhead**

A managed service might cost significantly more per unit than running the underlying software yourself. But if the managed service lets a small team launch a feature in hours instead of weeks, that difference can be irrelevant during the early stages of a company.

This is particularly important when the product itself is still changing rapidly.

You don't necessarily know which features will become critical infrastructure. Spending weeks building a highly optimized internal system for a feature that might be abandoned next month is usually a poor trade.

Managed services buy you something extremely valuable at this stage:

**optionality.**

You can ship the feature, observe how customers use it, and decide later whether the economics justify replacing the managed service.

## Start Managed, Then Take Control

This leads to an approach I increasingly like:

**Start with managed infrastructure. Replace it when the economics justify doing so.**

Early on, you might have:

```text
Terraform
   │
   ├── AWS managed services
   ├── SaaS providers
   └── Managed AI services
```

As the product matures, some of those services may become expensive enough to justify taking ownership:

```text
Terraform
   │
   ├── AWS managed services
   ├── ECS
   ├── EC2
   ├── Lambda
   └── Self-hosted infrastructure
```

The important part is that Terraform makes this transition much easier.

Your infrastructure is already represented as code. You're not starting from scratch when you decide to change the implementation.

## The Engineering Team Should Grow With the Economics

There's an old principle behind growing engineering organizations that still applies:

> **If the cost of a managed service becomes higher than the cost of having an engineer manage the equivalent infrastructure, hire the engineer.**

Early in a company's life, engineering time is often the scarce resource.

Later, infrastructure spend can become the scarce resource.

At that point, hiring engineers to replace expensive managed services can make economic sense.

This is why I don't think "managed vs. self-hosted" is a philosophical decision. It's an economic one.

If AWS is charging you $500 per month for something that would cost $50 per month to run yourself but requires 20 hours of engineering work every month, keep the managed service.

If AWS is charging you $20,000 per month for something you can reliably operate for $2,000 per month with a fraction of an engineer, it's worth investigating the replacement.

## What I Wouldn't Outsource

There is one category where I am much more hesitant to rely on managed services:

**core business logic.**

Infrastructure is one thing. Your competitive advantage is another.

If a service contains logic that is central to your product, I want as much control over it as reasonably possible.

Managed infrastructure is great for things like:

* Queues
* Object storage
* DNS
* Basic messaging
* Authentication infrastructure
* Compute
* Networking
* Generic databases
* Monitoring primitives

But if a system increasingly contains the actual intelligence or workflow that differentiates the product, I would rather own that logic.

The more features that migrate into that core business system, the stronger the argument becomes for hiring engineers and bringing more of the implementation in-house.

## AI Is Where This Gets Interesting

One of the biggest cost-saving opportunities I've seen recently is around **AI infrastructure**.

Managed AI services can be incredibly convenient. They can also have enormous markups relative to the underlying infrastructure required to implement simpler AI workflows.

With the architecture I've described in previous articles, it's surprisingly easy to recreate some of these capabilities yourself.

For example, this year I built a relatively simple AI service that takes a video and:

1. Generates subtitles.
2. Creates dubbed versions in the original language and other languages.
3. Processes the resulting audio and video.
4. Encodes the output for modern HLS players.

The infrastructure cost for the workflow can be **a few dollars per job**.

Comparable managed services can charge **hundreds of dollars** for similar functionality.

That's an enormous difference.

The reason is that many AI products are ultimately compositions of relatively straightforward components:

```text
Video
  │
  ├── Transcription
  │
  ├── Translation
  │
  ├── Voice generation
  │
  ├── Audio/video processing
  │
  └── HLS encoding
```

Once you understand the individual components, you can assemble them yourself and control the infrastructure underneath them.

You don't necessarily need to build a massive AI company to provide an AI-powered feature.

Sometimes you just need a queue, some compute, a few models, object storage, and a well-designed pipeline.

## The Real Advantage Is Speed

This is ultimately why I like the Terraform + AWS combination so much.

It changes what it means to know cloud infrastructure.

You don't need to memorize every AWS configuration option. You need to understand the concepts well enough to know **which primitive solves your problem**.

Terraform handles the repeatability.

AWS provides the building blocks.

Managed services let you move quickly.

And as the product grows, the same infrastructure-as-code foundation gives you a path toward replacing expensive services with your own infrastructure.

The goal isn't to self-host everything.

The goal isn't to use managed services for everything either.

The goal is to **spend engineering time where it creates the most value**.

At launch, that usually means letting someone else manage the infrastructure.

As the company grows, it may mean hiring engineers to take some of that infrastructure back.

And for core business logic, it means knowing when convenience has turned into a dependency you no longer want.
